Design lessons from basketball

I’ve played basketball longer than I’ve designed products, and I keep noticing the same lesson showing up in both.

Good teams don’t run their best play every possession. They run whatever the defense gives them, and they’ve practiced enough options that reading the situation is faster than forcing a plan. Bad teams have one plan and get visibly frustrated when it doesn’t work.

I think about that a lot when a design direction I’m attached to doesn’t survive contact with research. The instinct is to run the play harder — more polish, more conviction, a stronger pitch. Usually the better move is the same one a good point guard makes: read what’s actually in front of you and adjust, without treating the adjustment as a loss.

The other lesson is about repetition nobody sees. Free throws are won in practice gyms, alone, thousands of times, and nobody in the stands ever finds out how many hours went into that one clutch shot. Most of the design decisions people compliment in a shipped product were rehearsed a dozen unglamorous times first — wireframes nobody saw, copy that got rewritten six times, a flow that got simplified after a testing session that didn’t go well.

The parts of the craft that don’t make it into the case study are usually the parts that made the case study possible.
