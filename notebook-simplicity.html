Why simplicity is difficult

Simplicity gets talked about like an aesthetic choice. In practice, it’s closer to a very expensive form of honesty.

Every complicated interface I’ve ever inherited had a reasonable explanation for every individual piece of complexity. Nobody adds a field, a toggle, or a screen for no reason — a stakeholder asked for it, an edge case demanded it, a deadline made the simple version impossible that sprint. Complexity rarely arrives all at once. It accumulates, one defensible decision at a time.

That’s why simplifying something after the fact is so much harder than designing it simply from the start. You’re not fighting one bad decision. You’re negotiating with a dozen good ones, made by different people, for different reasons, none of which feel safe to remove in isolation.

On Payvice, removing three tabs down to one unified view meant convincing a team that every one of those tabs had once solved a real problem, and finding out whether that problem still existed or had just never been revisited. Most of it hadn’t been revisited in over a year.

The uncomfortable part of simplicity is that it requires someone to take responsibility for a "no" that has no single bad guy behind it. It’s much easier to keep every field than to be the person who says a specific, useful thing no longer earns its place. Simplicity isn’t the absence of decisions. It’s the accumulation of a lot of small, slightly uncomfortable ones.

Simplicity is not the starting point. It’s what’s left after everything indefensible has been removed.
