Building products with AI

AI hasn’t changed what good product design means. It’s changed how quickly bad decisions become expensive.

Every few years a new capability shows up and the same conversation happens: does this change design? My honest answer, after two years of shipping AI-assisted features, is no — and also, completely, yes.

It doesn’t change what good design means. Clarity, trust, respecting someone’s time and attention — none of that is up for renegotiation just because a model is doing more of the work under the hood. What it changes is the cost of getting those things wrong.

A traditional feature that misunderstands a user fails predictably. An AI feature that misunderstands a user can fail confidently — it will present a wrong answer with the same polished tone as a right one. That asymmetry is the actual design problem, and it’s mostly invisible in a demo.

On Tryblie, the daily digest that flags disengaged members is, under the hood, doing something you could call AI. We spent far more design time on how it communicates uncertainty — "hasn’t posted in 9 days, used to post weekly" instead of a confidence score — than on the underlying detection logic itself. The model can be right eighty percent of the time and still be a great feature, if the interface never asks the creator to trust it more than eighty percent.

The pattern I keep coming back to: design AI features the way you’d design for a very capable, occasionally wrong intern. Give them real responsibility, but build the checkpoints where a human catches the twenty percent before it costs something. The technology keeps changing. That instinct hasn’t needed to.

The interface should never ask someone to trust the model more than the model deserves. — from a talk I gave at a Perxels cohort review
