Case Study 01

Tryblie: building a home for close-knit communities

Tryblie — community platform

Industry

Community

Platform

Web & Mobile

Role

Product Design, Research

Timeline

2024 – Present

Overview

Tryblie is a platform that helps creators run paid, close-knit communities without the feeling of managing a spreadsheet of subscribers. I joined as the founding product designer and have shaped the product from its first sketch to the version people pay for today.

Most community tools ask creators to choose between intimacy and scale. A group chat feels personal but falls apart past a few hundred members. A course platform scales but feels transactional. Tryblie's bet is that you can keep the warmth of a small group while growing it — if the product does the organizational work quietly, in the background, instead of asking the creator to do it by hand.

The Challenge

Creators who tried to monetize a community were stitching together four or five tools — a Discord server for chat, a spreadsheet for members, a payment link for billing, an email tool for announcements. Every new tool added friction, and every seam between tools was a place where a member could quietly churn without anyone noticing.

The challenge wasn't to build a better Discord. It was to design a single home where paying members felt seen, and where the creator could understand the health of their community at a glance, without needing to become a part-time community manager.

Context

I came in six weeks after the founders had validated demand through a manual, concierge version of the product — running a handful of communities themselves using existing tools, charging for access, and taking notes on every point of friction. That manual version was the brief. My job was to turn a spreadsheet-and-Discord operation into software, without losing what made it work.

Understanding the Users

I spoke with fourteen creators across three categories — fitness coaches, indie educators, and niche hobbyist communities — plus twenty-two of their paying members. Two things came up consistently.

  • Creators didn't want more analytics dashboards. They wanted to know which members were quietly disengaging before they cancelled, not after.
  • Members didn't want more notifications. They wanted to feel like the creator actually knew they were there.
I don't need a dashboard. I need to know who's about to leave, and I need to know before they do. — Fitness coach, 640 paying members

Research

I mapped every existing tool each creator used, and where members actually spent their time versus where creators assumed they spent their time. The gap was significant: creators over-invested in polished welcome sequences and under-invested in the ongoing, unglamorous work of noticing when someone had gone quiet.

Research Finding
On average, creators discovered a disengaged member 11 days after their last meaningful interaction — usually at the moment of cancellation, when it was already too late to act.

Problem Definition

The core problem wasn't a missing feature. It was a missing signal. Creators needed a lightweight, ambient sense of community health that didn't require them to go looking for it.

Opportunity Statement

How might we give creators a felt sense of their community's health — who's thriving, who's fading — without turning community management into a second job?

Exploration

I explored three different directions: a traditional analytics dashboard, an automated nudge system that messaged members directly, and what we ultimately built — a quiet daily digest that surfaced three to five members worth a creator's attention, with just enough context to make a two-minute personal check-in feel effortless.

The dashboard direction was rejected early. It solved the problem for creators who already had time to spare, which wasn't our user. The automated nudge direction was rejected because it removed the creator from the relationship entirely — the thing members said they valued most.

User Flows

The flow that mattered most wasn't onboarding — it was the creator's daily loop: open Tryblie, see who needs attention, act on it, move on. I mapped this as a two-minute ritual, not a dashboard session, and every screen was designed against that time budget.

User flow — daily creator check-in loop

Wireframes

Early wireframes tested a card-based digest against a simple ranked list. The ranked list won decisively in testing — creators wanted to scan, not browse, and cards read as more effort than the two-minute window justified.

Low-fidelity wireframes — member digest exploration

Design Decisions

  • The digest, not the dashboard. We deliberately did not build a full analytics suite for version one, even though it was frequently requested. It would have solved a problem creators didn't actually have time to engage with.
  • Context over data. Each flagged member came with one line of plain-language context — "hasn't posted in 9 days, used to post weekly" — rather than a chart, because creators needed to act, not analyze.
  • The creator stays the messenger. Tryblie drafts a suggested check-in message but never sends it automatically. The relationship stays human.

High Fidelity Designs

The final interface leans on generous whitespace and a restrained type system so that, even as the product has grown to include events, paid tiers and richer messaging, the daily digest still reads as the calm center of the product rather than one dashboard among many.

High-fidelity UI — creator dashboard & member digest

The daily digest, member profile, and community settings, shown in the current production build.

Testing

We ran the digest with eight creators for six weeks before wider release. The clearest signal: creators who used the digest daily reported feeling more confident about their community's health, even when they took no action at all — the ambient awareness itself was the value, not just the resulting check-ins.

Reflection

The biggest lesson was resisting the instinct to build the feature creators asked for and instead building the feature that solved what they were actually struggling with. A full analytics dashboard would have shipped faster and satisfied more feature requests. It also would have failed, because it asked creators to do more work at exactly the moment they had the least time to give.

Lessons Learned

  • Ambient awareness can be more valuable than actionable data, if the data arrives at the moment it's needed rather than requiring a search.
  • Removing a requested feature is sometimes the strongest design decision available.
  • Designing around a real time budget — two minutes, not twenty — produced sharper decisions than designing around a feature list.
