The future of XR

Most XR demos are impressive for thirty seconds and exhausting after five minutes. I think that’s a design failure, not a hardware one.

I’ve spent the last year prototyping in XR outside of client work, mostly because the medium keeps forcing me to unlearn habits I didn’t know I had. Every screen-based instinct — hierarchy through position, attention through contrast, navigation through back buttons — has to be rebuilt from nothing.

The industry’s current answer to that problem is mostly to borrow 2D patterns and float them in 3D space. Menus that behave like phone menus, just further away. It works, in the sense that it’s legible. It also wastes almost everything that makes the medium interesting.

What’s stuck with me from a year of prototyping: the moments that felt genuinely new weren’t the ones with the most visual spectacle. They were the small interactions where physical intuition did the work an instruction screen normally would — reaching for an object instead of tapping a button, feeling for a boundary instead of reading a warning.

I don’t think XR needs better headsets before it needs better restraint. The products that will make this medium feel inevitable, rather than novel, are probably going to look boring in a screenshot — because their best qualities won’t be visible until you’re standing inside them.
